Brain structures, keyed to your chart numbers

Each structure keeps the number it has on your printed PaRama Brain Psychology and Physiology chart, so you can move between the two. The functions listed here are the scientific ones, taken from the sources at the bottom of the page.

The chart's own descriptions, including its planetary, S-function and R-function layer, are not reproduced here; read those on the printed chart. The figures are simplified schematics drawn for this page, not to scale. Chart numbers 22, 24 and 26 weren't legible in the photos, so they're left out.

Cerebral cortex

The folded outer grey matter, divided into lobes by landmark grooves.1

17Frontal lobe

  • The precentral gyrus is the primary motor cortex; its neurons command the spinal cord to move skeletal muscles.1
  • The premotor area plans movements, and the frontal eye fields drive eye movements and attention to visual stimuli.1
  • Broca's area produces speech and in most people is on the left side only.1
  • 2023The motor strip isn't one continuous body map: foot, hand and mouth zones alternate with regions of a separate action-control network. Other labs dispute parts of this interpretation.12

18Prefrontal cortex

  • Supports cognitive functions that can underlie personality, short-term memory and consciousness.1
  • 2024Works as a partner of the mediodorsal thalamus (T14), with which it is connected in both directions, for executive control.10

19Parietal lobe

  • Contains the primary somatosensory cortex (Brodmann areas 1–3): touch, pressure, tickle, pain, itch and vibration, plus the sense of body position and movement.1
  • Also helps process complex visual information.1

20Occipital lobe

  • Brodmann areas 17 and 18 carry out primary visual perception.1
  • Receives visual input relayed by the lateral geniculate nucleus (T2).2

21Temporal lobe

  • Primary hearing sits in Brodmann areas 41 and 42.1
  • Its limbic parts establish long-term memories.1
  • Receives auditory input relayed by the medial geniculate nucleus (T6).2

15Corpus callosum

  • The white-matter bridge that is the main communication route between the two hemispheres.1

Limbic system

A group of structures involved in emotion, memory and behaviour. It is no longer thought to be the only emotion system in the brain.6

16Cingulate gyrus

  • A paired structure on the inner surface of each hemisphere, best known as part of the limbic system.4
  • Connects with reward centres, the amygdala, lateral prefrontal and parietal cortex, motor areas, the spinal cord and the hippocampus.4
  • Its links with the hippocampus and amygdala suggest roles in consolidating long-term memory and processing emotional stimuli.4

27Septal region

  • A small area in the medial wall of the hemisphere, extending into the base of the septum pellucidum.8
  • Receives input from the hippocampus (via the fornix), the amygdala, the ventral tegmental area and the hypothalamus.8
  • Animal work links it to reward; human clinical evidence is limited, though rage behaviour has been reported in a few patients with midline strokes here.8
  • The medial septum modulates learning and memory through its projections to the hippocampus; the lateral septum has been linked to social behaviour, anxiety and fear.9

25Mammillary bodies

  • A core node of the Papez circuit: the hippocampus reaches them via the fornix, and they project to the anterior thalamic nuclei (T5).7
  • Important for consolidating memory; lesions cause major cognitive deficits.7
  • Damaged in thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, causing Korsakoff amnesia.11

3Amygdala

  • An almond-shaped group of about 13 nuclei in the temporal lobe, under the uncus, sorted into five functional groups.5
  • Involved in emotion, behaviour and forming memories; it links prefrontal-temporal cortex with the hypothalamus.5

23Hippocampus

  • With the amygdala and nearby cortex, forms long-term memories and emotional responses.1
  • 2025Dividing neural progenitor cells were found in the dentate gyrus of adults, confirming ongoing neuron formation; numbers varied widely between people.13

Basal ganglia

Deep nuclei that compare cortical activity with the body's overall state to decide whether a movement goes ahead.1

89Caudate nucleus, head and tail

  • A long, C-shaped nucleus running from the frontal lobe into the temporal lobe; the head is at the front and the tail curves down into the temporal lobe.1
  • With the putamen, it forms the striatum, which receives all cortical input to the basal ganglia.1

7Putamen

  • Lies deep under the front parts of the frontal and parietal lobes and is part of the striatum.1

5Globus pallidus

  • Sits just medial to the putamen and has an external and an internal segment.1
  • In the direct pathway, the striatum inhibits the internal segment, which releases the thalamus and increases cortical activity.1

4Subthalamic nucleus

  • A relay in the indirect pathway (striatum → external globus pallidus → subthalamic nucleus → internal globus pallidus / substantia nigra), which keeps the thalamus inhibited.1
  • Sends excitatory glutamate signals to the internal globus pallidus and substantia nigra.3

6Substantia nigra

  • The pars compacta releases dopamine into the striatum and acts as the switch between the direct and indirect pathways.1
  • Parkinson's disease results from these dopamine neurons dying.1

Diencephalon

The connection between the cerebrum and the rest of the nervous system; its two main parts are the thalamus and hypothalamus.1

1Thalamus

  • Every sense except smell passes through it before reaching the cortex, and it processes that information rather than just passing it on.1
  • Has about 60 nuclei; see the thalamus section below for the ones on your chart.2

2Hypothalamus

  • Regulates homeostasis and directs the autonomic and endocrine systems through the anterior pituitary; parts of it are involved in memory and emotion.1
  • 2025The HYPOMAP atlas charted over 450 cell types in the human hypothalamus, which controls sleep, body temperature, hunger and thirst, and showed differences from mice that matter for obesity drugs.16

Brain stem

12Brain stem, overall

  • Made up of the midbrain, pons and medulla; it connects the brain to the spinal cord.1
  • The cranial nerves connect here, and the main pathways between spinal cord and brain pass through it.1
  • 2024A wakefulness network with nodes in the brain stem, hypothalamus, thalamus and basal forebrain was mapped in human brains.15

14Midbrain

  • The superior colliculus combines visual, auditory and body-space information to orient the eyes; the inferior colliculus is part of the hearing pathway.1
  • 2024Its dopamine-producing ventral tegmental area is a hub linking the wakefulness network to the cortical default mode network.15

11Pons

  • The main connection between the brain stem and cerebellum; it passes descending forebrain input on to the cerebellum.1
  • Together with the medulla, regulates heart and breathing rates.1

13Medulla

  • Its outer white matter is continuous with the spinal cord, and its grey matter processes cranial nerve information.1
  • The reticular formation running through the brain stem relates to sleep, wakefulness and attention.1

Cerebellum

10Cerebellum

  • Compares the cerebrum's motor commands with sensory feedback and sends corrections when a movement is off; about 10% of brain mass.1
  • The anterior lobe mainly handles sensorimotor processing; the posterior lobe supports cognition and emotion.14
  • Posterior lobe damage causes the cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome, affecting executive function, visuospatial processing, language and emotional regulation.14

Thalamus nuclei

T numbers match the Thalamus panel of your chart. Each side of the thalamus has anterior, medial and lateral groups separated by the internal medullary lamina, plus thin midline nuclei and an outer reticular nucleus.2

T5Anterior nuclear group

  • Receives the mammillary bodies' output as part of the Papez circuit.7
  • Considered critical for episodic memory in humans.11

T14Medial dorsal (mediodorsal)

  • Connected both ways with all areas of the prefrontal cortex; a 2024 review assigns it a specific role in executive control.10
  • Causally involved in fast learning, working memory and adaptive decisions in primates and rodents.10

T13Midline nuclei

  • Thin nuclei next to the third ventricle, including the paraventricular, parataenial and rhomboid nuclei.2
  • Their functions aren't detailed in the sources used here.

T9Intralaminar nuclear group

  • Sit inside the lamina; they take input from the basal ganglia and project to the cortex.2
  • The central medial nucleus in this group handles alertness, motor information, consciousness and awareness.2

T7Centromedian

  • One of the caudal intralaminar nuclei.2
  • Its specific function isn't detailed in the sources used here.

T8Reticular nucleus

  • Wraps around the lateral thalamus and projects back onto the thalamus rather than to the cortex, regulating its activity.2
  • Receives input from the reticular activating system and basal forebrain, giving the thalamus a role in alertness and attention.2

T3T10Lateral dorsal and lateral posterior

  • Both belong to the lateral nuclear group of relay nuclei.2
  • Their specific functions aren't detailed in the sources used here.

T1Pulvinar

  • The largest thalamic nucleus, strongly connected with visual cortex and part of the visual attention network; visual salience is likely one of its main functions.17
  • One-sided pulvinar damage can cause neglect of the opposite side of space.17
  • 2023A review describes it as a hub for attentional modulation, feature binding and predictive coding.18

T4Ventral anterior

  • Part of the lateral nuclear group.2
  • Its specific function isn't detailed in the sources used here.

T11Ventral lateral

  • Receives input from the cerebellum and basal ganglia and sends motor information to the motor cortex.2

T16Ventral intermedial

  • Not covered in the sources used here.

T15Ventral posterolateral (VPL)

  • Relays body temperature and pain, vibration, pressure, fine touch and proprioception to the somatosensory cortex.2

T12Ventral posteromedial (VPM)

  • Relays facial sensation and taste to the somatosensory cortex.2

T2Lateral geniculate body

  • Receives input from the optic tracts and sends it on to the primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe.2

T6Medial geniculate body

  • Relays hearing from the superior olive and inferior colliculus to the auditory cortex of the temporal lobe.2
